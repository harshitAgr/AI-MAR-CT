 1. **ReMAR-DS: Recalibrated Feature Learning for Metal Artifact Reduction and CT Domain Transformation** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*Mubashara Rehman, Niki Martinel, Michele Avanzo, Riccardo Spizzo, Christian Micheloni* \
**Summary** \
ReMAR-DS is a 2D encoder-decoder network that jointly performs metal artifact reduction and image-domain transformation from kilovoltage CT (kVCT) to megavoltage CT (MVCT) for radiotherapy re-planning, taking 512x512 kVCT slices as input and producing artifact-corrected, MVCT-domain-consistent slices as output; the encoder builds hierarchical features via Conv-BN-ReLU stages and Enhanced Residual Blocks (EnResB) that use depthwise separable convolutions to cut parameter count, skip connections are routed through Recalibrated concurrent Spatial and Channel Squeeze-and-Excitation (RcsSE) blocks that generate sigmoid spatial-attention maps (via 1x1 convolution) and channel-wise recalibration (via global average pooling and FC layers) to focus reconstruction on artifact-corrupted regions, and the bottleneck latent representation is perturbed with learnable-scale Gaussian noise (sigma_L) as a regularizer, with input-level noise sigma_k=0.01 also injected during training; the decoder upsamples through CBR layers to restore resolution. Training is fully supervised on paired kVCT (32-slice Toshiba Aquilion) and MVCT (Hi-Art II TomoTherapy) scans from 52 cancer patients at Centro di Riferimento Oncologico di Aviano IRCCS, patient-wise split 70/30 train/test, with an artifact-heavy subset (HU > 2000 in kVCT) comprising ~14.78% of slices; the loss combines a weighted L1 term (weight 100), SSIM/MS-SSIM, and a Focal Frequency Loss for high-frequency fidelity, optimized with AdamW (lr 1e-4, halved every 20 epochs, weight decay 5e-4, batch size 4, up to 100 epochs with early stopping at patience 15) using flip and affine augmentation, on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with a 188 GB-RAM Xeon host. On the full test set, ReMAR-DS achieves PSNR 30.690 dB / SSIM 0.759 (computed within clinician-defined ROI masks), outperforming MAR-DTN (30.02 dB / 0.73), AttU-Net (30.12 dB / 0.73), Pix2Pix (28.7 dB / 0.69), and SwinIR (29.00 dB / 0.66); ablations show removing the RcsSE blocks drops performance to 30.410 dB / 0.751 and removing the noise regularization to 30.322 dB / 0.747, while on the artifact-heavy subset alone the full loss configuration reaches 27.670 dB / 0.703 SSIM versus 27.260 dB / 0.700 for weighted-L1-only. Per-image inference time is not reported. The authors note the ground-truth MVCT data used for training/evaluation is itself not entirely artifact-free, and flag future work on improving contrast consistency, refining HU accuracy, and extending the approach from 2D slices to full 3D volumes. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19531)] ICIAP, 2025. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-10192-1_18)]

 1. **Deep Learning-Based Metal Artifact Reduction in CT for Total Knee Arthroplasty (KMAR-Net)** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*J. Lee, H.-D. Chae, H. Cho, J. M. Kim, S. H. Hong, J.-Y. Choi, H. J. Yoo, S.-J. Ye* \
**Summary** \
KMAR-Net is a supervised, image-domain CNN designed specifically for post-operative knee CT after total knee arthroplasty (TKA), where large high-atomic-number femoral and tibial implants produce severe streak artifacts that are difficult for generic MAR methods to handle. Unlike dual-domain networks, KMAR-Net does not require projection data at inference, does not use an explicit metal segmentation step, and does not rely on a-priori knowledge of implant shape — the metal-corrupted axial slice is fed directly to the network and the artifact-reduced slice is produced end-to-end, which sidesteps the secondary-artifact problem caused by segmentation errors in projection-completion pipelines. Training data is generated via a sinogram-handling simulation pipeline: axial knee CT scans from 50 patients without any metallic implant are used as artifact-free references, simplified virtual metal shapes mimicking commonly used TKA components (distal femur, proximal tibia, long-stem variants) are manually designed with randomised size, orientation and position, and each metal-inserted image is forward-projected, metal-trace-replaced, and back-projected to obtain a paired artifact/clean slice — 500 pairs per patient, 25,000 pairs in total, split 15,000 / 5,000 / 5,000 for training / validation / simulated-test. A 44-patient clinical test set with prior TKA (18 unilateral, 26 bilateral) is used for the real-world evaluation, and a separate CTDI-phantom experiment (stainless-steel and aluminium rods, varying count, tube voltage 100/120 kVp, slice thickness 1/2 mm, bone and standard kernels) with 474 simulated pairs (330 / 72 / 72) plus 80 real phantom images is used to check feasibility under controlled conditions. The network is implemented in PyTorch 1.1.0; architectural specifics and training hyper-parameters (loss, optimiser, learning rate, inference time, exact GPU model) are deferred to the paper's supplementary material, with source code released at github.com/ljm861/KMAR-Net. On the simulated test set KMAR-Net reaches Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) 0.995, PSNR 41.49 dB and SSIM 0.996 against the artifact-free reference. On the 44-patient clinical test set the area of dark streak artifacts (≤ −200 HU pixels within the knee outline) drops from 2,165.2±576.5 mm² (Non-MAR) and 1,554.6±566.3 mm² (O-MAR, the commercial projection-based orthopaedic MAR baseline) to 100.6±94.5 mm² (KMAR-Net); mean attenuation within the streaks rises from −724.9±71.8 HU (Non-MAR) / −424.2±59.4 HU (O-MAR) to −247.7±28.0 HU (KMAR-Net), and the intra-streak SD collapses from 295.7±11.7 / 218.8±35.4 HU to 68.5±50.3 HU — all differences significant at p < 0.001. On the phantom, KMAR-Net (PCC 0.994 / PSNR 39.64 dB / SSIM 0.988; mean HU 117.3 / SD 19.2) is the closest to the metal-free reference (mean HU 118.7 / SD 19.9) and clearly beats O-MAR (0.985 / 35.85 / 0.938 ; 108.2 HU / 49.1 SD) and Non-MAR (0.928 / 31.27 / 0.895 ; 94.2 HU / 182.9 SD). Two musculoskeletal radiologists graded KMAR-Net superior to O-MAR for overall artifact level and soft-tissue depiction on all 44 clinical cases (5-point Likert, all P < 0.001), with bone-conspicuity superiority significant for the less-experienced reader (P < 0.001) but not the 6-year reader (P = 0.080); pseudocement artifacts dropped from 37/44 (84%) in O-MAR to 16/44 (36%) in KMAR-Net (P < 0.001). The authors note several limitations: (1) a newly introduced band-like soft-tissue blurring appears in 27/44 (61%) of KMAR-Net cases (absent from O-MAR), concentrated where the original streak burden was most severe, and cannot be explained analytically — a fundamental drawback of data-driven MAR; (2) the sinogram-handling simulation does not fully reproduce real-world physics (cone-beam effects, tube-current modulation, scatter, non-linear partial volume), so there is a residual simulated-to-real domain gap; (3) the 44-case clinical cohort is small, female-dominant, and restricted to four implant families (Vanguard, Attune, LPS-Flex, LCCK), limiting generalizability across sex, anatomy and implant design; (4) the only comparison is against commercial O-MAR — no head-to-head with dual-energy, hybrid, transformer, or diffusion-based DL MAR methods. \
Scientific Reports, 2025. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-21012-7)] [![Open Access](https://img.shields.io/badge/Open%20Access-brightgreen.svg)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-21012-7) [![Code](https://img.shields.io/badge/Code-purple.svg)](https://github.com/ljm861/KMAR-Net)

 1. **FIND-Net: Fourier-Integrated Network with Dictionary Kernels for Metal Artifact Reduction** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*F. Tasharofi, F. Fan, M. Qahqaie, M. Thies, A. Maier* \
**Summary** \
FIND-Net is a supervised, image-domain deep-unfolding MAR network that extends DICDNet's convolutional-dictionary model with joint spatial / frequency-domain processing to address CNNs' limited receptive fields. The underlying physical model decomposes a metal-corrupted CT image Y into an artifact-free component X and an artifact term A = Σₙ Kₙ⊗Mₙ, where Kₙ are learnable dictionary kernels that encode metal-artifact patterns and Mₙ are their feature maps; the masked reconstruction objective min_{M,X} ‖I⊙(Y−X−A)‖²_F + λ₁P₁(M) + λ₂P₂(X) is unrolled into 10 iterative stages, each containing an M-Net (updates M via a proximal step) and an X-Net (updates X via a proximal step), both implemented as ResNets whose standard 3×3 convolutions are replaced by FE-ResBlocks (Frequency-Enhanced ResBlocks) built on GFFC — a modified Fast Fourier Convolution that splits channels into a Local Branch for spatial convolutions and a Global Branch that applies FFT → trainable Gaussian filter Gσ,c(u,v) = exp(−((D²−c²)/(D·σ+ε))²) → 1×1 conv → IFFT, with learnable σ and c giving per-layer adaptive frequency selection. The Global-Branch channel fraction α increases across stages from 0.0 to 0.8, so early stages emphasise local spatial refinement while later stages emphasise global frequency-domain artifact suppression; X⁽⁰⁾ is initialised with linear interpolation (LI). Training uses a multi-term stage-wise loss L = Σₛ ωₛ‖I⊙(X−X⁽ˢ⁾)‖²_F + γ₁Σₛ ωₛ‖I⊙(X−X⁽ˢ⁾)‖₁ + γ₂Σₛ ωₛ‖I⊙(Y−X−A⁽ˢ⁾)‖₁, combining a Frobenius reconstruction term with L1 sparsity and artifact-consistency terms. The network is trained on the AAPM CT-MAR Grand Challenge dataset (XCIST-simulated from NIH DeepLesion body scans and UCLH Stroke EIT head scans, with realistic quantum noise / beam hardening / scatter) at 512×512: 5,500 training cases (1,300 head + 4,200 body), 700 validation and 700 test cases, plus a 29-case real-world evaluation set without ground truth. Implementation is in PyTorch on an NVIDIA A100 (80 GB HBM2) with AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999), LR 1×10⁻⁴, weight decay 1×10⁻⁵, linear warm-up + cosine annealing, batch size 6, up to 200 epochs with early stopping. Across metal sizes FIND-Net reports average MAE/SSIM/PSNR of 17.9 / 0.952 / 42.8 dB, versus LI (26.6 / 0.913 / 38.9), DICDNet (18.3 / 0.951 / 42.4), OSCNet (18.8 / 0.948 / 41.7), OSCNet+ (18.8 / 0.948 / 42.0), and the FIND-Net (No-GF) ablation (18.2 / 0.951 / 42.5); the relative improvements over the DICDNet baseline — −3.07% MAE, +0.18% SSIM, +0.90% PSNR — are statistically significant at the 95% CI level. On seven artifact-free anatomical patches (where an ideal MAR should not change the image), FIND-Net achieves the best preservation (MAE 0.005 / SSIM 0.97 / PSNR 43.23 dB) vs. DICDNet-level baselines (~MAE 0.007 / SSIM 0.94 / PSNR 40.4 dB). Computational complexity drops ~16% (334.45 → 279.55 GFLOPs) via FFT-based operations, but per-image inference time grows from 0.15 s (DICDNet) to 1.07 s because of the frequency-domain transforms — the authors flag this latency overhead as the method's main practical limitation; they additionally observe that OSCNet / OSCNet+ underperform DICDNet on this benchmark, suggesting their rotational-symmetry encoding generalises less well than FIND-Net's frequency-adaptive formulation. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10617)] MICCAI, 2025. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-05169-1_19)] [![Open Access](https://img.shields.io/badge/Open%20Access-brightgreen.svg)](https://papers.miccai.org/miccai-2025/paper/1842_paper.pdf) [![Code](https://img.shields.io/badge/Code-purple.svg)](https://github.com/Farid-Tasharofi/FIND-Net)

 1. **Diffusion model-regularized implicit neural representation for computed tomography metal artifact reduction (INR-DR)** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Unsupervised-blue.svg" alt="Unsupervised"> \
*J. Wen, C. Du, X. Wang, Y. Zhang* \
**Summary** \
INR-DR is an unsupervised, per-case MAR method that fits an implicit neural representation (INR) of the artifact-free image to the metal-free part of the measured sinogram, regularized by a frozen DDPM pre-trained unconditionally on clean CT; only the INR is optimized and no paired data are needed. The INR FΦ maps a continuous 2D coordinate p = (x, y) to an attenuation value through a multi-resolution hash encoding (16 levels, 8 features per level, hash table size 2¹⁹) followed by a Tiny-CUDA-NN MLP with two 64-wide ReLU hidden layers and a linear output (11.19 M parameters). Each outer iteration has two phases. (1) Data fidelity: projections ỹ(r) = Σ FΦ(p)·Δp are computed as differentiable line integrals along fan-beam rays, and Φ is trained for 1000 epochs with a ray-averaged L1 loss |(1−Tr(r))⊙(ỹ(r)−y(r))|, where the metal trace Tr excludes every ray that intersects metal. (2) Diffusion regularization: the INR is discretized to an image x₀, noised to timestep t and denoised in one step with Tweedie's formula, and Φ is then fitted to the denoised image x̂₀ with a point-wise ℓ2 loss, so the prior reaches the next fidelity phase only through the INR's initial parameters. t decreases from 1000 in steps of 50 for coarse-to-fine regularization, with far fewer INR epochs once t < 200. The output is the INR image itself, so no sinogram stitching (a source of secondary artifacts) is needed. The DDPM is DuDoDp's, pre-trained on 927,802 artifact-free, mostly chest/abdomen DeepLesion images. Simulated DeepLesion tests use the standard protocol with CNN-MAR metal masks: 416×416 LAC images, fan-beam geometry with 640 views over 360° and 641 detectors, and 200 clean images excluded from DM training × 10 masks (35 to 2061 pixels) in five size groups. From the largest to the smallest metal group, INR-DR reaches PSNR/SSIM of 38.28/0.9789 to 44.32/0.9899, versus 37.74/0.9759 to 46.48/0.9927 for the supervised InDuDoNet+ (author checkpoint) and 35.39/0.9655 to 38.36/0.9779 for the unsupervised DuDoDp (same DM), with CNN-MAR, NMAR and LI lower in every group. INR-DR is therefore best on the largest metals and beats DuDoDp in every group. On clinical dental CT (metal thresholded at 2500 HU, sinograms forward-projected from the reconstructed images), assessed only visually against the same patient's metal-free post-operative scan, InDuDoNet+ and DuDoDp over-suppress tooth structure and add secondary artifacts between implants, while INR-DR preserves the teeth. Ablations: removing the diffusion prior costs 2.12 dB PSNR and 0.0057 SSIM and fails to recover vertebrae enclosed by a non-convex implant; DM-only iterative sinogram inpainting without the INR is 6.2 dB and 0.0206 worse, with dark streaks between bridging implants. On one NVIDIA RTX 4090, average inference takes 97.52 s (91.30 M parameters), against 42.31 s for DuDoDp, 1.65 s for InDuDoNet+ and 0.53 s for CNN-MAR. Limitations noted by the authors: the INR must be re-trained for every case at test time, so it is much slower than end-to-end networks; it trails the supervised InDuDoNet+ on in-domain small and medium metals (lower PSNR on the three smaller groups); and the chest/abdomen-trained DM cannot supply accurate structural priors for dental/head scans, serving only as a generic denoising regularizer. The formulation and all experiments are 2D. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08999)] Quantitative Biology, 2026. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/qub2.70031)] [![Open Access](https://img.shields.io/badge/Open%20Access-brightgreen.svg)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/qub2.70031)

 1. **A multi-stage deep learning framework for half-detector truncation and metal artifact reduction in CBCT (HD-TMAR)** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*Junhyun Ahn, Jongduk Baek* \
**Summary** \
HD-TMAR is a supervised three-stage sinogram-then-image framework for dental CBCT with a offset half-detector (HD) geometry, where truncation compounds metal artifacts. Stage I: the HD sinogram is reconstructed with FDK-W (FDK with Wang's weighting) into IHD and forward-projected in full-detector (FD) geometry to give a corrupted sinogram SFD. A Sinogram Refinement Module thresholds IHD at 1500 HU to obtain a metal sinogram Smetal and builds a prior image (LI plus Zhang et al.'s tissue processing) whose projection is lower-bounded at 0.1 × its maximum. Within the measured region, metal is subtracted rather than inpainted (Srefined = SFD − Smetal) and also normalised by the prior projection; the two form a 2-channel input to SinoNet, whose target is the measured region of the metal-free FD sinogram. Stage II: at inference SFD is split into two overlapping patches (measured region; truncated region plus overlap) processed independently by SinoNet, and a Sinogram Merging Module restores the original HD measurements outside the metal trace, blends the patches linearly over a 20-pixel overlap (the Wang-weighting region) and reconstructs with FDK, giving the intermediate HD-TMAR image. Stage III: ImgNet removes residual low-frequency artifacts, giving HD-TMAR+. Both networks share a modified U-Net with CBAM in every skip connection and a global residual connection, trained with MSE + λp·VGG-16 perceptual + λmt·metal-trace MSE (λp = 0.1, λmt = 10 for SinoNet; λp = 0.005, λmt = 0 for ImgNet). Data are simulated from eight metal-free TCIA CT volumes with patient-specific implant, crown and bridge masks built from segmented teeth and bone (40 configurations, 6,000 slices), using a 120 kVp polychromatic spectrum (gold, titanium, zirconium) and Poisson noise at 10⁵ photons; geometry is SID/SDD 350/560 mm, 512 views, FD 512×200 vs HD 266×200 detector (73.062 mm offset), and 512×512×150 volumes at 0.332 mm. The split is by patient (5 train / 1 validation / 2 test); training uses 256×256 crops, 200 epochs, batch size 16, Adam and LR 5×10⁻⁴ on an NVIDIA L40S. On 388 metal-affected test slices, HD-TMAR+ reaches PSNR/SSIM 35.51 dB / 0.9430 (37.24 / 0.9603 on 612 non-metal slices) and HD-TMAR 35.33 / 0.9380, vs LI-ConvNet 34.46 / 0.9330, CNNMAR 34.20 / 0.9219, DuDoNet 33.63 / 0.9290 and NMAR 32.71 / 0.9029; DuDoNet and DSCMAR were run in a single-row fan-beam setup because of GPU memory. In ablations, metal subtraction beats inpainting (35.33 vs 32.86 dB), overlapped patching with replacement beats direct full-sinogram inference (sinogram PSNR 44.95 vs 42.89 dB), and a self-supervised variant (NMAR pseudo-labels in the metal trace, loss only outside it) reaches 33.92 / 0.9226. Inference takes 12.15 ms per slice for SinoNet (6.6 GB peak memory) and 22.47 ms for ImgNet (12.1 GB), ≈5.8 s per volume (GPU not stated). Limitations noted by the authors: simulation-only evaluation; scatter or motion could violate the merging module's assumption that non-metal measurements are accurate; higher memory and runtime than single-stage methods; 2D slice-wise processing struggles with large cone angles; outer truncation (object beyond the FOV) is not handled; and task-based evaluation is still needed. \
Medical Physics, 2026. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/mp.70604)] [![Open Access](https://img.shields.io/badge/Open%20Access-brightgreen.svg)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13448102/)

 1. **Solving Energy-Independent Density for CT Metal Artifact Reduction via Neural Representation (Diner)** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Unsupervised-blue.svg" alt="Unsupervised"> \
*Q. Wu, X. Guo, L. Chen, Y. Liu, W. Ren, Y. Zhang, S. K. Zhou, J. Yu, Y. Zhang* \
**Summary** \
Diner (Density neural representation) is an unsupervised, per-scan implicit neural representation (INR) method that reconstructs a single energy-independent density map σ(x) from raw metal-corrupted projections, instead of energy-dependent linear attenuation coefficients (LACs). It decomposes μ(x,E) = γ(x,E)·σ(x), with γ the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC), and assumes water-equivalent tissue and a homogeneous metal whose MAC is known, giving μ(x,E) = σ(x)·{[1−M(x)]·γwater(E) + M(x)·γmetal(E)} for a binary metal mask M. The authors' earlier Polyner instead solves LAC maps at many energies with an energy-smoothness regularizer. An MLP gφ, built from multiresolution hash encoding and two 128-neuron fully connected layers with ReLU and ELU, maps a 2D coordinate to σ. Densities at points sampled every Δx along a ray are converted to LACs at N discrete energies, and a differentiable polychromatic forward model, ρ̂(r) = −ln Σᵢ η(Eᵢ)·exp(−Σₓ μ(x,Eᵢ)·Δx), gives the estimated projection; the normalized spectrum η is a known prior estimated with SPEKTR. The only loss is an L1 data-consistency term over 80 randomly sampled rays per iteration. The output is the LAC map at the equivalent monochromatic energy E⋆ = ⌊Σᵢ η(Eᵢ)·Eᵢ⌋. Optimization uses Adam (LR 1×10⁻³, halved every 500 epochs, 2000 epochs) and takes about 1.2 min on a single NVIDIA RTX TITAN GPU; hyper-parameters were fixed after tuning on 10 DeepLesion samples. Simulated tests use 50 DeepLesion and 20 LIDC 256×256 fan-beam slices with the ten metal masks of Zhang et al., three metals (titanium, chromium, 304 stainless steel), a polychromatic source, Poisson noise and the partial-volume effect. Real data are five mouse thighs with 304-stainless-steel intramedullary needles, scanned before and after needle removal on a Bruker SKYSCAN 1276 micro-CT, and a body phantom scanned with and without a Ti stick on a UIH uCT 768 helical scanner. Baselines are FBP, LI, ART, supervised CNN-MAR, DICDNet and ACDNet, unpaired ADN (author-released weights; all but CNN-MAR trained on DeepLesion) and Polyner. On DeepLesion titanium, Diner reaches PSNR/SSIM 39.17 dB/0.9846 vs ACDNet 38.43/0.9701, DICDNet 37.93/0.9710 and Polyner 37.23/0.9769. On out-of-domain LIDC with 304 stainless steel, ACDNet (26.84 dB) and DICDNet (28.10 dB) fall below LI (29.30 dB), while Diner obtains 33.43/0.9472 vs Polyner 31.37/0.9450. Real-data results are qualitative: Diner is closer to the needle-free reference than Bruker's NRECON, whose artifacts CTan counts as bone (inflating bone volume, BV/TV and trabecular number), and reduces artifacts relative to the vendor reconstruction on the phantom. In DeepLesion ablations, a linear-integral forward model drops PSNR from 39.19 to 34.47 dB, a random N(0,1) spectrum to 22.45 dB and Gaussian spectrum noise of 3% / 1.5% of the peak to 35.64 / 36.80 dB; a single energy bin (N=1) gives about 27 dB, while N=5–101 changes PSNR by less than 2 dB. Author-noted limitations: ultra-high-absorption metals such as gold cause photon starvation, and because Diner treats all measurements as valid it fails on them (16.70 dB vs 23.07 dB for ACDNet on simulated gold, both with severe shadows); it also needs raw projections and acquisition geometry, which commercial scanners may not expose. The method further requires a known spectrum, the metal MAC and a metal mask. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07047)] IEEE Transactions on Radiation and Plasma Medical Sciences, 2026. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/TRPMS.2026.3720818)]

 1. **DuDoCROP: Dual-Domain CLIP-Assisted Residual Optimization Perception Model for CT Metal Artifact Reduction** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*X. Zhang, A. Cai, S. Wang, L. Wang, Z. Zheng, L. Li, B. Yan* \
**Summary** \
DuDoCROP is a dual-domain diffusion MAR framework that conditions mean-reverting image-restoration SDEs (IR-SDE) on vision-language embeddings. Stage 1 (DuDoCLIP-assisted prior generation) fine-tunes DuDoCLIP, modelled on DA-CLIP: LLaVA-v1.5-13b, given "Qualifier + Instruction" prompts, describes clean images and clean sinograms, and describes the residual maps Xr = Xma − Xref and Yr = Yma − Yref as QSPD tuples (metal Quantity, Size and Position extracted from the mask, plus a LLaVA artifact Description). A frozen CLIP ViT-B-32 text encoder embeds the texts; a frozen CLIP image encoder and a trainable control net (a copy of the encoder joined by zero convolutions) map a metal-affected image or sinogram to an image embedding matched to the clean-content text and a control embedding matched to the tuple, trained with an InfoNCE-style contrastive loss over both domains. The embeddings guide two IR-SDEs, one restoring the image (Xma → Xp) and one the sinogram metal trace. Each is a conditional U-Net of ResBlocks and linear attention in which the image embedding enters bottleneck Spatial-Transformer blocks by cross-attention and the control embedding, added to the time embedding, scales and shifts ResBlock features; both use the IR-SDE maximum-likelihood L1 loss. Stage 2 (one-step residual optimization) merges the predicted trace with unaffected raw data, Yp = ε̂Θ1(Yma)⊙Ms + Yma⊙(1−Ms), reconstructs it by FBP as Xp′, and min-max normalises the image prior to the range of Xp′. A third, unguided IR-SDE, trained against Xp − Xref, refines the residual Xp − Xp′; the result is subtracted from Xp with weight k, re-normalised to X̂p and fused as Xo = α·X̂p + β·Xp′, with k, α, β ∈ [0, 1] hand-tuned. The four networks are trained sequentially. A proposed perceptual indicator (PI) measures the mean change in (local metal-region + global) LPIPS per unit change in metal count (PIq) or largest metal area (PIs); lower is better. Data come from the AAPM CT-MAR Grand Challenge (512×512 images, 1000×900 sinograms): 2,475 body + 528 head training pairs, and 864 official body (body1), 642 further body (body2) and 136 head test slices, plus 200 unlabelled clinical CTPelvic1K slices. DuDoCLIP uses batch 200 and LR 2×10⁻⁵; the IR-SDEs use 100 steps, 256×256 crops, batch 16 and AdamW (LR 2×10⁻⁴), training 400 (image) and 600 (sinogram) epochs in about 3 days on one NVIDIA RTX A6000 (48 GB). PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS×100 is 42.51/0.980/0.34 on body1 (InDuDoNet 41.88/0.947/2.44, ACDNet 41.84/0.942/2.82, DuDoDp 40.23/0.972/0.98), 43.58/0.980/0.37 on body2 and 44.05/0.994/0.37 on head (InDuDoNet 41.42/0.982/0.83); LI, CNNMAR, CycleGAN and ADN stay at or below 33.92 dB. Artifact Reduction** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*Z. Wu et al.* \
**Summary** \
PRAISE-Net is a deep learning framework for metal artifact reduction (MAR) in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), designed to ensure data consistency in the projection domain. It employs a Low2High strategy consisting of two stages. In the first stage, the Prior Information-Guided Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (PIG-DDPM) performs low-resolution inpainting of metal-corrupted regions. The inputs to this model are the projection data affected by metal artifacts and a prior estimate generated using linear interpolation; the target is the corresponding metal-free projection data. In the second stage, a Super-Resolution Reconstruction (SRR) module based on a Swin Transformer takes the inpainted low-resolution projections and reconstructs high-resolution projections. The target for this stage is the high-resolution metal-free projection data. To improve generalization to real clinical CBCT data, PRAISE-Net incorporates a CBCT Domain Adaptation (CBCT-DA) module into the training phase of PIG-DDPM. CBCT-DA uses two encoders to disentangle domain-invariant anatomical features from domain-specific features. It employs adversarial loss through a domain discriminator to align the feature distributions of simulated and clinical data, and uses mutual information loss to enforce separation between anatomical and domain-relevant representations. Importantly, CBCT-DA is used only during training to enable domain-invariant feature learning; it is not required during testing, where the trained model can be directly applied to clinical CBCT data. After high-resolution projections are restored, the final CBCT images are reconstructed using the Feldkamp-Davis-Kress (FDK) algorithm. \
IEEE Transactions on Instrumentation and Measurement, 2025. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIM.2025.3551446)]

1. **Radiologist-in-the-Loop Self-Training for Generalizable CT Metal Artifact Reduction** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Self-Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Self-Supervised"> \
*C. Ma et al.* \
**Summary** \
RISE-MAR is a novel radiologist-in-the-loop self-training framework for generalizable CT metal artifact reduction (MAR), addressing the domain gap and confirmation bias issues in existing supervised and semi-supervised MAR methods. The framework integrates a Clinical Quality Assessor (CQA)—a transformer-based network trained on radiologist-annotated CT images—that evaluates MAR outputs and ensures only high-quality pseudo ground-truths are used for training. CQA employs a spatial-frequency token mixer combining convolutional and self-attention mechanisms for multi-scale feature extraction, followed by a vectorization layer and quality head that predicts clinical quality scores. The self-training framework utilizes a teacher-student architecture, where the teacher network, pretrained on simulated MAR data, generates pseudo ground-truths for real clinical images. These pseudo ground-truths are assessed by CQA, and only high-quality ones (within a defined threshold) train the student MAR network via a supervised loss (L_sim) on paired simulated data and an unsupervised loss on real clinical images. The teacher model is progressively updated using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the student’s improved weights, ensuring continuous refinement of pseudo ground-truths. Extensive experiments on multiple clinical datasets demonstrate RISE-MAR's superior generalization over state-of-the-art methods in out-of-domain datasets.\
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.15610)]. IEEE TMI, 2025. [[doi](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/10857416)]

**Summary** \
This paper introduces a parameter-free metal-induced beam hardening correction (MBHC) method for CT imaging utilizing implicit neural representations (INRs). 1. **Sinogram-Image Dual-Domain Network for Robust Metal Artifact Reduction in CT Image (DSI-Net)** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*C. Liu, Y. Huang, B. Li, H. Ding* \
**Summary** \
DSI-Net is a supervised, end-to-end-trained dual-domain MAR network for 2D fan-beam CT. It runs two sinogram networks in parallel, one refining the LI residual to keep tissue detail (as in DAN-Net/DSCIP) and one treating the metal trace as missing data (as in DuDoNet), reconstructs each output with FBP, and fuses the two images with an image-domain U-Net. RS-Net (residual-based sinogram network) takes the attenuated residual Ssub = (Sma − SLI) × β between the metal-corrupted sinogram and its linear-interpolation (LI) version, with β between 0.3 and 0.5 to trade artifact suppression against peri-implant detail. Ssub is concatenated with the metal-mask projection Mp = FP(Xmetal) to offset trace information lost in down-sampling. Per Fig. 3(c), RS-Net is a Conv3×3 + LeakyReLU encoder–decoder with skip connections (32→64→128 channels, 640×641 down to 160×161) whose output is masked by the metal trace Mt and added to a global input skip to give Sres; the corrected sinogram is Ssc = Sma − Sres and Xsc = FBP(Ssc). LIS-Net (LI-based sinogram network) is a Conv3×3 + BN + ReLU U-Net (three down-sampling stages to 256 channels at 80×80) that takes [SLI, Mt], with a mask pyramid network (MPN) feeding down-sampled Mt into the encoder. Only the trace is replaced: Sse = Mt ⊙ LIS-Net([SLI, Mt]) + (1 − Mt) ⊙ SLI, and Xse = FBP(Sse). I-Net, a depth-4 U-Net, takes [Xsc, Xse] and predicts a residual: Xout = Xsc + I-Net([Xsc, Xse]). The loss is an unweighted sum of sinogram L1 terms on Ssc and Sse, an FBP loss (L1 on Xsc and Xse, added for geometric consistency) and an L1 loss on Xout, with the metal region excluded from all image terms; FP/FBP are differentiable ODL operators. Following the DSCIP protocol, 1,200 DeepLesion images and 100 CNNMAR metal masks give 90,000 training pairs (1,000 images × 90 masks) and 2,000 test pairs (200 × 10), simulated with beam hardening, Poisson noise and polychromatic X-rays (640 views over 360°, 416×416 images, 641×640 sinograms). Training uses Adam ((β₁, β₂) = (0.5, 0.999)), batch size 1 and an LR of 2×10⁻⁴ halved every 30 epochs, for 300 epochs on an NVIDIA TITAN RTX. On the synthetic test set (PSNR/SSIM averaged over four metal-size groups), DSI-Net reaches 43.85 dB / 0.9889, against ACDNet (43.05 / 0.9842), DuDoNet (42.13 / 0.9691), InDuDoNet+ (41.27 / 0.9701), DICDNet (40.90 / 0.9772) and LI (33.58 / 0.8607), all run from public code/models. In the ablation, RS-Net and LIS-Net alone reach 39.51 and 40.70 dB, and adding I-Net to each gives 42.94 and 43.59 dB. Visually, RS-Net alone leaves more artifacts and LIS-Net alone blurs edges; DuDoNet over-smooths and DICDNet leaves radial artifacts. On two CTPelvic1K clinical cases (metal thresholded at 2,500 HU; qualitative only), DICDNet and ACDNet blur tissue, InDuDoNet+ leaves streaks, and DSI-Net is reported to suppress artifacts better than DuDoNet. No inference time is reported. The method relies on accurate metal masks and traces: when masks are dilated with 3×3/5×5/7×7 MaxPool2d kernels, additional artifacts appear (shown only visually), which the authors attribute to imprecise masks. They also state that large or multiple metal objects need improvement, and leave clinical studies on real patient data to future work; quantitative evaluation uses only simulated data and PSNR/SSIM. \
ECAI, 2024. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.3233/FAIA240511)] [![Open Access](https://img.shields.io/badge/Open%20Access-brightgreen.svg)](https://ebooks.iospress.nl/doi/10.3233/FAIA240511)

1. **DiffMAR: A Generalized Diffusion Model for Metal Artifact Reduction in CT images**  <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*T. Cai et al.* \
**Summary** \
DiffMAR frames CT metal artifact reduction as reversing a physically motivated *linear degradation* process that mimics metal-artifact formation, and learns an iterative restoration path with a conditional diffusion model trained on paired metal-corrupted/metal-free CT images. To reduce error accumulation across the multi-step reverse process, it introduces a Time-Latent Adjustment (TLA) module that adaptively adjusts time embeddings at the latent level. To better preserve anatomy (and avoid over-smoothing), it adds a Structure Information Extraction (SIE) module that extracts structural priors from the linearly-interpolated (LI) correction in the image domain and injects them at each restoration step. On a DeepLesion-based synthetic benchmark (1,000 training images; 90 training masks; 2,000 test images; images resized to 416×416 with 640 views) and clinical testing (SpineWeb plus an in-house dental CT set), DiffMAR outperforms LI, DuDoNet, InDuDoNet(+), CycleGAN/Pix2PixGAN, and DICDNet/ACDNet, improving average PSNR/SSIM from 45.31 dB / 0.9899 (ACDNet) to 46.28 dB / 0.9922 and lowering RMSE from 0.0133 to 0.0119 on the synthetic test set. \
IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics, 2024. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/JBHI.2024.3439729)]

1. **MEPNet: A Model-Driven Equivariant Proximal Network for Joint Sparse-View Reconstruction and Metal Artifact Reduction in CT Images** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*H. Wang, M. Zhou, D. Wei, Y. Li, Y. Zheng* \
**Summary** \
MEPNet is the first deep-unfolding network designed for the **joint sparse-view reconstruction + metal-artifact reduction (SVMAR)** problem — a setting motivated by the fact that real low-dose protocols already under-sample projections, and metal implants make those sparse-view artifacts substantially worse. The optimisation problem is written as min_{S̄,X} ‖𝒫X − Ȳ⊙S̄‖²_F + λ‖(1 − T_r ∪ D)⊙(Ȳ⊙S̄ − Y_svma)‖²_F + μ₁R₁(S̄) + μ₂R₂(X), where 𝒫 is the forward Radon operator, Y_svma is the sparse-view metal-affected sinogram, T_r is the metal trace, D is the binary mask of *missing* projection views (sparse-view sampling), Ȳ = 𝒫X̃ is the normalisation coefficient produced from a prior image X̃, S̄ is the normalised clean sinogram and (1 − T_r ∪ D) is the data-fidelity mask that excludes both metal traces *and* the missing-view bins so the residual is computed only on observed, non-metal sinogram entries. A two-step proximal-gradient algorithm is unrolled into K=10 stages: each stage runs an S̄-net that updates the sinogram by descending the data-fidelity gradient and applying prox_{μ₁η₁}, then an X-net that back-projects the residual through 𝒫^T and applies prox_{μ₂η₂}. The S̄-net's proximal operator is a standard CNN (four [Conv + BN + ReLU + Conv + BN + Skip] residual blocks), but the X-net's proximal operator is the paper's central contribution: a **rotation-equivariant CNN** built on the p8 group (8 discrete in-plane rotations with shared parameters) whose 2-D filters are written as ψ(x) = Σ_{m=0..p-1}Σ_{n=0..p-1}[a_{mn}φ^c_{mn}(x) + b_{mn}φ^s_{mn}(x)] in a Fourier-series basis with p=5, so the same learnable coefficients {a_{mn}, b_{mn}} reproduce the filter under all 8 rotations via π_θ[ψ](x) = ψ(U_θ^{-1}x). This explicit rotational prior matches the rotational symmetry of CT scanning (the same organ is imaged from different angles in any sparse-view sub-sampling), so the X-net learns a representation that is geometrically aligned with the data instead of having to discover the symmetry from scratch. Trained parameters are the proxNet weights {θ_{S̄}^(k), θ_X^(k)}_{k=1..K}, the prior-image learner θ_prior, the step sizes η₁, η₂ and the fidelity weight λ. Training uses the same staged proxNet loss as InDuDoNet and is implemented in PyTorch with Adam (β₁=0.5, β₂=0.999), initial LR 2×10⁻⁴ halved every 40 epochs, batch size 1, 100 epochs, on an NVIDIA Tesla V100-SMX2 GPU. Data follows the InDuDoNet+ DeepLesion synthesis: 1,000 clean abdomen/thorax CT images × 90 metal masks for training and 200 clean CT × 10 metal masks (sizes [2061, 890, 881, 451, 254, 124, 118, 112, 53, 35] pixels) for the DeepLesion test set (2,000 pairs at 416×416 with 640 fully-sampled views in 360°). Sparse-view rates are simulated by uniformly sub-sampling 80, 160 and 320 of those 640 views — i.e. ×8, ×4 and ×2 dose-reduction factors. Cross-dataset evaluation uses Pancreas-test (50 pairs from 5 patients) and CLINIC-test (3,397 pairs from 10 volumes with one test metal). Quantitative results (PSNR dB / SSIM, sub-sampling rate ×8 / ×4 / ×2) — DeepLesion: MEPNet **38.48/0.9767, 41.43/0.9889, 42.66/0.9910** vs InDuDoNet 37.50/0.9664, 40.24/0.9793, 40.71/0.9890, DuDoNet (sparse-view-aware variant) lower across the board, FBPConvNet 25.91/0.8467, 27.38/0.8851, 29.11/0.9418 and raw input 12.65/0.3249, 13.63/0.3953, 16.55/0.5767; Pancreas: MEPNet 36.76/0.9726, **40.69/0.9872, 41.17/0.9896** (× 4 and × 2 best, marginal -0.10 dB at ×8 vs InDuDoNet); CLINIC: **39.04/0.9654, 41.58/0.9820, 42.30/0.9857** vs InDuDoNet 38.39/0.9572, 39.67/0.9621, 40.86/0.9811. The DeepLesion ×4 by-metal-size breakdown (Large→Small) gives MEPNet 37.51/0.9797, 39.45/0.9879, 42.78/0.9920, 43.92/0.9924, 43.51/0.9924 — average **41.31/0.9889**. Crucially, MEPNet hits these numbers with **4,723,309 parameters** vs InDuDoNet's 5,095,703 (a 7.3% reduction) — the rotational prior actually *reduces* the number of free parameters needed because the same Fourier-series coefficients reproduce 8 rotated copies of each filter, so the equivariance both improves accuracy and shrinks the model. The InDuDoNet baseline in Table 1 doubles as an implicit ablation: it is structurally identical except for the equivariant-CNN proxNet, so the consistent multi-dB gain across three datasets and three sparsity rates is attributable to the rotation prior alone. Limitations explicitly noted by the authors: (1) all training and test pairs are produced by the standard simulation protocol, leaving a simulated-to-clinical domain gap that the paper does not close — they call out collecting clinical sparse-view metal-implanted scans as future work; (2) inference time per image is not reported in the paper, so the practical speed cost of the rotation-equivariant proximal net relative to InDuDoNet's plain CNN proxNet is not quantified; (3) only the p8 group at p=5 is evaluated — there is no systematic sweep over rotation order or basis order, so whether finer rotation discretisations (p16) or higher-order Fourier series help on small implants is left open; (4) the joint SVMAR formulation requires both T_r and D to be known at inference, so any error in metal segmentation or in the assumed sparse-view sampling pattern will leak into the data-fidelity term. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14274)] MICCAI, 2023. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-43999-5_11)] [![Code](https://img.shields.io/badge/Code-purple.svg)](https://github.com/hongwang01/MEPNet)

1. **InDuDoNet+: A Deep Unfolding Dual Domain Network for Metal Artifact Reduction in CT Images** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*H. Wang, Y. Li, H. Zhang, D. Meng, Y. Zheng* \
**Summary** \
InDuDoNet+ is the journal-extension of InDuDoNet that keeps the 10-stage dual-domain deep-unfolding backbone (alternating S̃-net and X-net solved by a proximal-gradient unfolding of the joint-regularised problem min ‖PX−Ỹ⊙S̃‖²_F + α‖(1−T_r)⊙(Ỹ⊙S̃−Y)‖²_F + λ₁g₁(S̃) + λ₂g₂(X)) but replaces the original heuristic U-shape Prior-net with a **knowledge-driven Prior-net** that embeds CT-physics priors into the normalisation coefficient Ỹ, substantially improving interpretability, parameter efficiency and cross-dataset generalisation. The new Prior-net works in two steps: (i) a classical thresholding/k-means clustering pipeline on the LI-corrected image X_LI produces a coarse tissue segmentation X̃_c by forcing air → −1000 HU, soft tissue → 0 HU and bone → original values (metal value arbitrary since only sinogram bins close to — not inside — the metal trace contribute after normalisation); (ii) a shallow **WNet** of only three Conv+BN+ReLU layers then refines X̃_c pixel-wise into the final prior image X̃, from which Ỹ = P X̃ is obtained by forward projection. In parallel the paper introduces a **channel-wise concatenation/detachment** mechanism: each proxNet at stage n receives not just the single-channel update Ŝ_{n−1} (or X̂_{n−1}) but also an auxiliary variable Q^s_{n−1} (or Q^x_{n−1}) of dimension N_s (or N_x), set to 32, expanding the input to N_b×N_p×(1+N_s) and letting the proximal networks propagate richer iteration-wise information; the proxNets themselves are four [Conv + BN + ReLU + Conv + BN + Skip] residual blocks with 3×3 kernels. Initial states S̃₀, X₀, Q^s₀, Q^x₀ are produced by ResNet-structured proxNet_{θ^(0)} modules applied to the LI inputs together with learnable 3×3×32 convolutional filters. Training uses the same staged MSE loss as InDuDoNet (β_N=1, β_n=0.1 for n<N, γ=0.1). Data: synthesised DeepLesion with fan-beam geometry, polychromatic X-ray, partial-volume effect, beam hardening and Poisson noise — 1,000 clean CT × 90 metal masks for training, 200 CT from 12 patients × 10 metal masks for test (2,000 pairs, 416×416 images, 640 views, 641×640 sinograms); a cross-body-site Synthesised-Dental set; and clinical SpineWeb vertebrae images for visual evaluation (2,500 HU threshold for clinical metal masks). On the synthesised DeepLesion benchmark (by metal size, Large → Small) InDuDoNet+ reports PSNR/SSIM of 36.28/0.9736, 39.23/0.9872, 41.81/0.9937, 45.03/0.9952, 45.15/0.9959 — average **41.50 dB / 0.9891**, with the lowest average RMSE 16.93 HU among all compared methods: Input 27.06/0.7586/73.33 HU, LI 29.27/0.9347/53.37 HU, NMAR 29.48/0.9442/50.08 HU, CNNMAR 30.38/0.9644/45.69 HU, DuDoNet 31.14/0.9814/43.96 HU, DSCMAR 33.21/0.9375/32.16 HU, DuDoNet++ 39.69/0.9886/27.78 HU, original InDuDoNet 41.48/0.9904/18.20 HU. Although the PSNR/SSIM numbers are essentially tied with InDuDoNet, the standard deviation of PSNR drops from 4.73 to 4.37 dB and the standard deviation of SSIM from 0.0088 to 0.0066, i.e. InDuDoNet+ is **more stable across metal sizes**; the knowledge-driven Prior-net is also lighter (the WNet has only 3 convolutional layers, vs the 4-level U-shape in InDuDoNet), which the authors argue reduces overfitting and improves cross-dataset generalisation to Synthesised-Dental and clinical SpineWeb. An ablation with "InDuDoNet w/o Prior-net" (the unfolding without normalisation) gives 40.18/0.9876 average PSNR/SSIM at only 1.74 M parameters, validating that most of the gain beyond DuDoNet++ comes from the physics-geometry unfolding itself rather than the Prior-net alone, while the Prior-net is responsible for the remaining stability margin on large implants. The authors note several limitations: (1) evaluation on clinical data is qualitative only, since no paired artifact-free ground truth exists; (2) clinical metal masks at inference still rely on a simple 2,500-HU threshold, which can propagate segmentation errors; (3) the hand-crafted thresholding step inside the knowledge-driven Prior-net can be inaccurate for atypical tissue distributions, and the WNet refinement is only three convolutional layers, so it may not fully correct such errors; (4) as in InDuDoNet, the unfolding depth is capped at N=10 because deeper unfolding invites gradient vanishing. \
[[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.12660)] Medical Image Analysis, 2023. [[doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.media.2023.102729)] [![Code](https://img.shields.io/badge/Code-purple.svg)](https://github.com/hongwang01/InDuDoNet_plus)

**Summary** \
InDuDoNet is a supervised deep-unfolding dual-domain MAR network that explicitly bakes the CT imaging geometry into the architecture so that each layer corresponds to a specific operation in an iterative optimisation algorithm, sacrificing none of the data-driven prior fitting of CNNs while recovering model interpretability that pure black-box MAR networks lack. The method formulates MAR as a joint sinogram / image reconstruction problem: given the metal-affected sinogram Y, binary metal trace T_r, a Radon operator P and a normalisation coefficient Ỹ (forward projection of a prior image X̃), the clean sinogram is written as S = Ỹ⊙S̃ and the objective becomes min_{S̃,X} ‖PX − Ỹ⊙S̃‖²_F + α‖(1−T_r)⊙(Ỹ⊙S̃ − Y)‖²_F + λ₁g₁(S̃) + λ₂g₂(X); the prior regularisers g₁ and g₂ are learned implicitly via CNN-based proximal operators rather than hand-crafted. A proximal-gradient algorithm that uses only simple operations (point-wise multiplication, forward/backward projection) alternately updates S̃ and X; N=10 iterations of this algorithm are unfolded into N=10 stages of the network, each stage containing an S̃-net (computes Ŝ_{n-1} per Eq. 7 and passes it through proxNet_{θ_s̃^(n)}) and an X-net (computes X̂_{n-1} per Eq. 9 and passes it through proxNet_{θ_x^(n)}). Both proxNets share the same structure — four [Conv + BN + ReLU + Conv + BN + Skip] residual blocks. A separate U-shape Prior-net is trained to generate the normalisation coefficient Ỹ from the concatenation of the metal-affected image X_ma and its LI-corrected counterpart X_LI; X₀ is initialised by passing X_LI through a dedicated proximal network proxNet_{θ_x^(0)}. The ablation over stage count shows that N=0 (X_LI only) gives 33.54/0.9521, N=6 gives 41.00/0.9899, N=10 is best at 41.48/0.9904, and N=12 actually dips slightly (41.64 PSNR but 0.9897 SSIM) because the deeper unfolding starts suffering from gradient vanishing; the authors pick N=10 for the PSNR/SSIM/parameter sweet spot. At N=10 the full network has only 5.17 M parameters — fewer than DSCMAR (25.83 M) and DuDoNet++ (25.98 M) — and takes 0.5116 s per 416×416 image on V100 (N=6 takes 0.3526 s with 4.54 M parameters). On the CLINIC-metal downstream segmentation task, InDuDoNet raises the average Dice across sacrum / left hip / right hip / lumbar spine to 0.9490 vs 0.9478 (DuDoNet++), 0.9469 (DuDoNet), 0.9396 (DSCMAR), 0.9367 (CNNMAR), 0.9303 (NMAR), 0.9263 (LI) and 0.9245 (raw input). The authors' own limitations noted in the paper: (1) N=12 underperforms N=10 due to gradient vanishing, so deeper unfolding past 10 stages is not beneficial without additional stabilisation; (2) clinical metal masks at inference are produced by simple HU-thresholding at 2,500 HU, so segmentation errors on the metal mask can propagate into the MAR result; (3) all training data are synthetic DeepLesion pairs, leaving a simulated-to-clinical domain gap that is only indirectly validated through the pelvic-segmentation downstream task rather than with paired clinical ground truth. \

**Summary** \
The authours proposed a deep learning-based framework to enhance CT MAR by leveraging information across multiple Hounsfield Unit (HU) windows. They argued that traditional approaches typically normalize CT data into a single HU window, which can compromise training effectiveness due to unequal emphasis on different HU ranges—an important consideration in clinical applications where tissues are best visualized under specific window settings.  Multiple Window Learning Network (MWLNet) addresses this by employing multiple convolutional neural network (CNN) branches, each tailored to a specific HU window. Inter-branch communication is facilitated by Window Transfer (WT) layers, which normalize and clip image data to map across HU ranges, enabling end-to-end training with gradients flowing between branches to enforce learning across scales. MWLNet was evaluated on simulated CT scans of spine, teeth, and hip regions, as well as clinical datasets, with all images being 512×512 slices exhibiting varying artifact levels. Results showed that MWLNet outperformed NMAR and single-window CNN baselines (SWLNets) in PSNR, SSIM, and visual quality, notably preserving fine structures in narrow windows while reducing artifacts more effectively. Intermediate results validated that contextual information from larger HU windows improves prediction accuracy in smaller windows.

1. **Deep-MAR: Fast Enhanced CT Metal Artifact Reduction Using Data Domain Deep Learning** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*M. U. Ghani, W. C. Karl* \
**Summary** \
Deep-MAR formulates CT metal artifact reduction as **non-blind sinogram completion** and trains a conditional GAN (CGAN) to inpaint the metal trace directly in the projection domain — the completed sinogram is then reconstructed with conventional filtered back-projection (FBP), making the method a drop-in pre-FBP module that fits into existing scanner workflows without modifying the reconstruction pipeline. The generator G is an encoder–decoder CNN (with BatchNorm, leaky-ReLU in the down-path, ReLU + transposed-convolutions in the up-path, dropout after every BN layer in the final block to limit overfitting) that takes a metal-trace-masked input sinogram and outputs only the correction inside the metal region via the residual form G(x) = x + M ⊙ x_D1, where M is the binary metal mask and x_D1 is the last-layer feature: this preserves non-metal sinogram samples exactly and focuses the capacity on the missing data. The discriminator D operates on the **full sinogram** (not patch-based) because metal-corrupted missing-data regions follow a coherent sinusoidal geometry that local patches cannot capture; for speed, D runs on 2×-downsampled sinograms. The training objective combines a standard CGAN adversarial loss (weighted by λ=10) with an L2 reconstruction term on the completed sinogram. To handle the data scarcity of the security-CT application that motivated the work, the authors build a two-tier training regime with **transfer learning**: (1) a large simulated corpus is generated via a physically-accurate Imatron-C300-style X-ray simulator (poly-energetic source with N=121 uniformly sampled energies between 10–130 keV, detector response g^(i)=2.6×10⁻³·E^(i) pA/quanta, source intensity I₀=1.7×10⁵ photons/ray, electronic noise variance σ_e²=3.37 pA², Beer-Lambert line integrals with NIST XCOM attenuation coefficients, parallel-beam 720 views × 1024 detector channels zero-padded to 1024×768 sinograms, 475×475 mm² FOV) coupled with a **stochastic bag simulator** that randomly places up to 5 metallic / non-metallic objects per scene; this yields 10,000 base scenes × (up to 5 metals/scene) = 50,000 simulated sinogram pairs plus 25 hand-crafted cylinder/sheet "suitcase" scenes; (2) a smaller real set of 1,706 metal-free slices from a real Imatron C300 is augmented by virtually deleting up to 5 metal regions per scene, yielding 8,530 real training pairs used for transfer-learning fine-tuning. Horizontal-flip augmentation is used on both sets, the ASTRA toolbox handles the forward projections, and test sets comprise 510 simulated pairs (102 scenes × up to 5 metal layouts) plus 159 real pairs from ALERT Task Order 4 for quantitative evaluation. Optimisation uses Adam (LR 2×10⁻⁴, β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999) with mini-batch size 6 over 25 epochs on the simulated set, and in the first 4 epochs the discriminator takes 6−k extra steps per generator update (k=1..4) to stabilise training. On the 510 simulated test images Deep-MAR achieves **MSE 1.2×10⁻⁴, SSIM 0.9941, PSNR 60.32 dB (units cm⁻¹)**, vs Uncorrected 3.9×10⁻² / 0.7205 / 33.72 dB, LI-MAR 4.7×10⁻⁴ / 0.9750 / 54.67 dB, and WNN-MAR 5.5×10⁻⁴ / 0.9713 / 53.81 dB — a 74% MSE reduction over LI-MAR and 78% over WNN-MAR. On the 159 real test pairs the transfer-learning model reaches MSE 5.8×10⁻⁵ / SSIM 0.8840 / PSNR 33.91 dB vs LI-MAR 1.4×10⁻⁴ / 0.8158 / 30.49, WNN-MAR 1.8×10⁻⁴ / 0.8029 / 29.61, and a from-scratch Deep-MAR 7.5×10⁻⁵ / 0.8605 / 32.68 — a 59% MSE reduction over LI-MAR, 68% over WNN-MAR, and a 23% relative MSE improvement of transfer learning over training from scratch, directly demonstrating the sim-to-real benefit. Inference is fast: the generator performs sinogram completion in **~58 ms on an NVIDIA Tesla P100** GPU, while the (unoptimised) CPU FBP step takes ~1.55 s per slice on an Intel Xeon 2.4 GHz, Linux, MATLAB R2017b. Limitations explicitly noted: (1) the method restricts itself to 2-D parallel-beam geometry; extension to fan/cone-beam is left to future work; (2) photon scatter is not modelled in the simulator (Monte-Carlo scatter is O(n⁶) and would prohibit the large simulated corpus) — the authors argue a simpler ray-based simulation suffices for sinogram completion training but note this would need revisiting for scatter-heavy clinical CT; (3) metal segmentation at inference relies on a 4,000-MHU threshold followed by erosion/dilation with radius-2/4 disk structuring elements — segmentation errors therefore propagate directly into the completion; (4) quantitative real-data evaluation uses virtually inserted metals because true before/after matched pairs are not physically obtainable in security scanners, which is a practical but not strictly ideal surrogate. \

1. **DuDoNet: Dual Domain Network for CT Metal Artifact Reduction** <img src="https://img.shields.io/badge/Supervised-blue.svg" alt="Supervised"> \
*W.-A. Lin, H. Liao, C. Peng, X. Sun, J. Zhang, J. Luo, R. Chellappa, S. K. Zhou* \
**Summary** \
DuDoNet is the first end-to-end trainable dual-domain network for CT metal artifact reduction: it simultaneously enhances the metal-corrupted sinogram **and** the reconstructed image, and introduces a differentiable **Radon Inversion Layer (RIL)** that allows gradients to back-propagate from the image domain back into the sinogram domain during training — enabling the two branches to learn jointly rather than in two isolated stages. The pipeline has three components. (1) **SE-Net** (Sinogram Enhancement Net): takes the LI-repaired sinogram Y_LI and a metal-trace mask M_t as inputs and outputs a refined sinogram Y_out via a mask-pyramid-augmented U-Net, where the mask pyramid propagates metal-trace information across multiple scales so that small metal implants, whose trace vanishes in deep feature maps, are still respected — ablation shows the mask pyramid adds ~0.2 dB for small metals without hurting large-metal performance. (2) **RIL** (Radon Inversion Layer): converts the enhanced sinogram from fan-beam to parallel-beam, applies Ram-Lak filtering, and back-projects to the image X̂, so that the pipeline can compute a Radon-consistency loss L_RC directly in image space. (3) **IE-Net** (Image Enhancement Net): a residual U-Net that fuses X̂ and the LI-reconstructed image X_LI, outputting X_out = X_LI + G_i(X̂, X_LI). The full objective is L = L_Gs + L_RC + L_Gi with L_Gs an L1 on the enhanced sinogram, L_Gi an L1 on X_out, and L_RC the Radon-consistency term (the authors deliberately keep all three coefficients equal to 1 and report that tuning them is unnecessary). An interesting deliberate design choice is that DuDoNet refuses to take the raw metal-corrupted sinogram/image as input — it was experimentally found that feeding the raw inputs instead of LI-corrected ones leaves metal shadows and streaks uncorrected, so both branches start from the LI-repaired baseline. Data follows the DeepLesion synthesis protocol: 4,000 training CT images from 320 patients × 90 metal masks = **360,000** training combinations; 200 test CT × 10 metal masks = 2,000 test pairs; training metal sizes 16–4,967 pixels, test sizes 32–2,054 pixels. All CT images are resized to 416×416 and sinograms to 321×320 (320 projection views uniformly in 0–360°, source-to-rotation-center 39.7 cm), with polychromatic X-ray spectrum, 2×10⁷-photon Poisson noise and partial-volume effect simulated. Training is in PyTorch on **two NVIDIA 1080Ti** GPUs using Adam (β₁=0.5, β₂=0.999), batch size 8, initial learning rate 2×10⁻⁴ halved every 30 epochs, for 380 epochs total. On the DeepLesion synthetic test set (PSNR / SSIM by metal size, Large → Small): LI 20.20/0.8236, 22.35/0.8686, 26.76/0.9098, 28.50/0.9252, 29.53/0.9312 (avg 25.47/0.8917); NMAR avg 27.51/0.9001; cGAN-CT avg 28.07/0.8733; RDN-CT avg 31.74/0.9156; CNNMAR avg 29.52/0.9243; **DuDoNet** 29.02/0.8972, 31.12/0.9256, 34.32/0.9481, 36.72/0.9595, 36.36/0.9592 (avg **33.51 dB / 0.9379**). Ablation quantifies every contribution: SE-Net alone 26.46–26.71 avg PSNR, IE-Net alone 31.45, SE-Net + IE-Net (no RC loss) 32.95–32.96, and adding the Radon-consistency loss lifts this to 33.41–33.51 (+0.5 dB on average, +0.3 dB on every metal size), validating that RIL-enabled gradient flow between the two domains is essential. Qualitative comparisons on three metal-size regimes show that NMAR, cGAN-CT, RDN-CT and CNNMAR still leave shadowing or speckle noise in the middle / between implants, while DuDoNet cleanly removes both shadows and streaks and recovers fine organ details even for two-implant cases. The authors note several implicit limitations: (1) feeding the raw metal-corrupted sinogram/image directly does not work — DuDoNet needs a pre-processing LI step as input, making it a refinement rather than a fully end-to-end metal handler; (2) evaluation is synthetic-only in the main paper (clinical results are in supplementary material), with no quantitative clinical ground-truth metrics; (3) there is no explicit per-image inference time or parameter count reported in the main text beyond the dual-GPU training configuration. \
